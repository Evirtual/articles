2,684 characters

Over the last two weeks I had several AI agents working on the same codebase at once. They were fast. Then someone asked me whether the work was done, and I realised I could not answer without taking an agent's word for it.

I couldn't check 143 models by hand, so I spent twelve days building something that could: a ledger. Every model has to clear it before approval, and the last check, the visual one, is mine. Eleven checks, one page that says which of them hold right now and which went stale.

Four rules did most of the work.

→ A count has to balance. Every model sits in exactly one stage, the parts of a stage add up to the stage, and the build records whether it balanced. "It is like an accountant" is the note I sent, and it stuck.

→ A result goes stale when what it judged changes. Not on every commit — the check fingerprints what it actually looked at, including shared code and its own rule version. A pass under rule v1 never counts as a pass under v2.

→ "Didn't run" is never "passed". A crashed test gets its own bucket, named, and it blocks.

→ A check nobody has seen fail is decoration. Every check ships with a deliberately broken copy it must fail.

What that caught, in the first six days: 35 models with text below WCAG AA on one stage; 18 that only looked right because the site's stylesheet set box-sizing for them; MP4 exports coming out dark because the video stream carried no colour description; a click that piled up elements until the page froze; a zoom feature measuring 926 element boxes per keystroke.

Then the second week: eight checks that had been passing turned out to have holes in what they were asked — lettering that never arrived, a pass recorded three days earlier, a check comparing a machine to itself. The fix was not more checks. It was making every result say which files it judged, and making the push actually refuse when the page said it was blocked.

What it could not catch: a planet's ring that didn't wrap the planet, a sun spilling below the horizon, a rocket that read as shrinking instead of launching. All three passed every check that existed. I found them by looking.

Cost, honestly: about 9.6 billion tokens in the first six days, a weekly allowance gone by Tuesday, two collisions where two agents edited one file and one of them lost, a gate that once had 57 headless browsers open and made me restart the laptop, and background waiters left running for 25 hours.

If you are running agents in parallel and the building is fast but nobody can tell you the state, feel free to DM me. I am open to consulting and product/engineering advisory work.

The whole process, mistakes included, is in the article below.

Twelve days · 949 commits

A green tick is a date, not a fact

Twelve days building the page that refuses to tell me a model is fine — and the eight times it said so anyway. Counted from git, the checks and the session logs.

Edgaras Neverdauskas22 min read5,150 words

Projectcss3dlab.edgarasneverdauskas.com ↗

What it is for

CSS 3D Lab is a gallery of 143 three-dimensional models — a rotating cube, a flip clock, a Newton's cradle, a model train — drawn with no WebGL and no 3D library. Each one is ordinary HTML, and the depth comes from CSS transforms. 99 are HTML and CSS alone; in the other 44 a little JavaScript feeds numbers in and CSS still does all the drawing. You can look at any of them, open the code, edit it live, copy it as one file, and export it as a picture, a video or a printable sheet.

I built the first version with Claude in about a day, and it was fine. Then I wanted one more feature — the export — and it cost twelve days, a rulebook, eleven automated checks, 1,287 separate verdicts and a page built to hold all of it. This is the story of the page.

Here is why a download button costs that much. To put a model in a file, something has to decide what the model should look like, and the moment I looked, no two places agreed. The bookshelf looked right in its gallery card; opened full screen, one book's cover hung off the shelf; exported, it moved again. One model, three places, three slightly different bookshelves. Nearly every model had some version of it, most too small to complain about. A few pixels. Nobody files a bug about a few pixels, which is exactly what makes them expensive — and a picture is a receipt. Once somebody downloads it, the drift is permanent and it has their name on it.

So the job became: make every model look the same everywhere, and be able to prove it. The proving turned out to be the hard part, and the page that does the proving is called the Ledger.

Act one: the week I built the Ledger, which takes nobody's word

Sunday: eleven hours on one panel

The first day went entirely on the panel where you choose a shape and a size and take a picture of a model. Eleven hours. One panel. Message by message, me describing what was wrong and Claude fixing it.

That day cost 1.61 billion tokens, and only 238 million of them were an agent doing work. The rest was conversation — me explaining, it replying, and both of us re-reading the whole discussion on every turn. I did not know that at the time; you only see it later, in the logs.

Monday 10:23: somebody writes the rule down

Monday started by taking out the fixes that had been made by hand — a nudge here to stop something sticking out there. A model held together by nudges cannot be checked, because there is no rule it is supposed to follow. So at 10:23 the rule went into a file: one page saying what every model must do. Size itself against the space it is given rather than to a fixed number; never be too small or spill over its edges; stay centred; look the same in a card, in the editor, full screen and in a picture.

Then things started coming out of the project, fast.

  • Monday morning
  • 10:23the rule is written down
  • 10:31hold a model's height, not its shape — and delete the second way of doing it
  • 10:39retire the measuring run entirely
  • 10:57the frame is the canvas: take the placement machinery out
  • 11:01add the check that judges models instead of quietly adjusting them

That last line is the whole project in one commit. Until then, code had been correcting models on their way to the screen. After it, nothing corrected anything — a model either satisfied the rule or it was wrong, and something said so.

In the afternoon the rule was handed to twelve Claude agents at once. They rewrote every model in the gallery against it: four and a half hours, 253 commits, a hundred of them in the three o'clock hour. That afternoon cost about a billion tokens, roughly what Sunday had cost. Sunday produced one panel. This produced the gallery. At 14:28 the machine started complaining — "my laptop started to lag and heat too much, let's reduce the number" — which is the part nobody mentions about running twelve agents. They are not free, and the thing they cost is the computer you are sitting at.

Monday 14:42: "where can I see what is happening?"

By mid-afternoon I had a problem that had nothing to do with code. Twelve agents were finishing work. Each one told me it was done. I had no way of knowing whether any of them were right, and no way to see all of it at once. I was reading commit messages and taking their word for it.

So at 14:42 I asked where I could see what was happening. At 15:25 it was live: one page, every model a row, every check a column, rebuilding itself as results arrived.

One rule sits behind it, and everything else follows from that one rule. A result expires when the thing it judged changes. Change a model's code and every verdict about that model goes grey and has to be earned again. Nobody can approve something into being finished. You cannot argue with it, and neither can I.

Tuesday: the day the money ran out

Tuesday was the quietest day and possibly the most useful: more lines came out of the project than went into it — the last hand-made fixes, and the status notes and little scripts the page had made pointless. The checks themselves were written in the small hours: stages at 02:54, share previews at 03:19, pause and keyboard access at 03:42.

It was also the day the subscription ran out.

18:14 — "please don't use too much credit for this now, I am at 95% of weekly."

That one is my favourite, and not because it is dramatic. It is tacked onto the end of a message about where a panel should sit. Worry about the bill and one more design change, in the same breath. Tuesday ended on 143 commits. Monday had ended on 391. There was not less to do. I was counting every message.

Wednesday 20:37: two agents, one file

Twice that week, two agents worked on the same file at once, and one of them lost. The first was at 18:30, and the recovery commit does not soften it: "My three commits before this one were spliced from the working tree against the wrong base." The second I watched happen live: the ledger's page updated, then reverted to an older design, then updated again. Not a crash. Not a warning. Two agents each make a sensible change, each reports success, and the second quietly writes over the first. Both told me the truth. The work was gone anyway.

The fix is not clever coordination. It is giving each agent its own files and, where that is impossible, telling one of them in writing not to touch the other's. The release checklist opens with a group called Nothing is lost, and one of its items exists only because of that evening.

Thursday: the night the checks ran

Checking a model means opening a browser, loading it, measuring it and closing it again. For 135 models and eleven checks, that is a great many browsers. At one point a run opened 57 at once and the laptop had to be restarted. Twice.

The fix was a limit of two browsers at a time. It turns a run that took minutes into one that takes hours, and it is the difference between a check you can leave going overnight and a check that takes your computer away from you. That night it ran unattended for hours and cost 214 million tokens — about a quarter of what one evening of moving a progress bar around had cost earlier in the week.

Friday 09:24: the gate holds — and 19:08: sixteen fixes with no gate at all

On the fifth morning the full run printed the line the whole week had been aimed at: GATE HOLDS: every model held every check. Some of what it caught I would have shipped without blinking. A ring of text that didn't wrap its planet. A sun below its own horizon. And the bookshelf.

The branch went up at 19:47, green in four minutes and forty-two seconds. Then somebody opened the export panel on the live site and, in about ten minutes, found something that 135 models and eleven checks had not: a payment card whose long number sat neatly inside the edge on my laptop and hung off the end on the live site. The other computer that draws the pictures did not have the same lettering, and used something very slightly wider. Every check had passed, because every check had only ever asked my machine to draw the picture. That is not a check that failed. It is a check nobody wrote, which is worse, because a gap makes no noise.

Here is the part I am least comfortable writing down. Between 19:08 and 23:29 that Friday, sixteen commits went straight to the live site, and not one of them went through the gate. The gate took two and a half hours; the site was broken in front of somebody using it; so I fixed the thing, checked the one thing I had changed, and pushed. At 22:17 I pushed a layout change. At 22:19 I reverted it. I had built a careful process for the calm part of the week and abandoned it for the part where it would have mattered most — and the person taking things on trust, on Friday night, was me.

What I needed did not exist: a way to ask one question about one model and get an answer in a minute. The gate could only answer everything at once, which means that when you are in a hurry, it answers nothing.

Act two: the week the ticks lied to me, politely

Eight checks, in eight days, that had been passing. Not one of them had a bug in what it measured. Every one had a hole in what it had been asked.

The lettering that was never there

We started sending the lettering along with the model, so the other computer would draw with the same font. Two nights later the numbers still hung off, and what was actually happening is the funniest thing I found all week. The lettering lives in one folder at the top of the site. But the code asked for it from wherever you happened to be standing — and you are always standing on some model's own page. So it went looking for lettering inside the bookshelf's folder. There has never been any lettering in there.

On my own machine it was worse. Ask my development server for a file that doesn't exist and it politely hands back the website's home page instead, while saying "found it". The code had a check for exactly this: if the answer isn't OK, stop. The answer was OK. So the home page got packed up and sent as the lettering.

A week of "the exported picture doesn't match", a whole system built to send the lettering, eighty lines of styling rewritten — and none of it had ever arrived. The part I keep thinking about is that check. If the answer isn't OK, stop is a real check. It is the one you would write. And it could never once have caught this, because the thing it guards against says OK.

A pass recorded three days earlier

A check failed on a model. I went looking for what had broken it, and found that the model's previous result said pass — recorded three days earlier. Nothing had broken it. It had been failing for three days. The board was green because nothing had asked the question again since Wednesday, and a tick sitting in a file has no idea what day it is.

The expiry rule only fires when something changes. A model nobody touches keeps its tick for ever, and for ever is a good deal longer than a tick stays true. The page now fades a tick as it ages — full colour the day it ran, almost grey a fortnight later. It doesn't expire it and it doesn't fail it. A board that turned red every time you went on holiday would only teach you to ignore red. It took about fifteen lines. The date had been sitting in the file the whole time.

The check that covered six per cent of its name

The export row said most of its models had "passed on older code", and I assumed the page was confused, because a moment earlier the export step had reported no problems at all. The page was not confused. I was. There are two ways the exports get checked: one goes over all 135 models at their default settings, the other takes a sample of eight and tries every setting. The overnight run was supposed to do the first. It was doing the second — nobody had handed it the list of models, so it quietly fell back to its sample and reported that as the step. So 127 of 135 export verdicts were from two days earlier, before every font change of the week, and exports are the exact thing those font changes affect. Every time I said the font problem was fixed, I was standing on eight models and calling it a hundred and thirty-five.

The page had been saying so the whole time, in a colour meaning "this was true about older code". I looked straight at it and decided the page was wrong.

The lettering had never once arrived

The Saturday story has a second half. Resolving the lettering against the right folder was correct reasoning and a correct commit message. It shipped doing nothing, for a reason that has nothing to do with the first one: the build tool rewrites "find this file, next to this one" into a lookup table, and it wrote the table with one set of names and read it with another. Every lookup missed. What came back was nothing, which the server answered with the home page. Two independent bugs, one symptom, a week apart.

The answer turned out to be four characters. Every font file begins with the letters wOF2. You do not ask whether the answer was OK. You look at what came back and check it is a font. With that in place, the eight failures I had been staring at all week resolved into one cause, and five models went green that had been red since Thursday.

The check that only ever asked itself

Then the address of the service that draws the exports for real visitors arrived, with one line: local and the workers should be the same export results. Until then nothing had ever asked it anything. The export check compared the downloaded file against what the dialog shows — and both of those are drawn on my laptop, by the same machine, out of the same font folder. It had been comparing something to itself all week and reporting agreement with complete confidence.

Seven of the charts named no font that ships with the file, so the far machine drew them in whatever it happened to have. That is the original bug — the one the lettering was added to fix — still alive on day three, under a row of green ticks. A check that compares two things on one machine cannot see a difference that only exists between machines. There is now a step that asks the other renderer the same question, and it is part of the gate.

The gate: four hours, and the best red of the week

Sunday night I set the whole thing running and went to bed. Four hours and two minutes; one red. compare — the step that asks whether an export draws what the screen shows — failed after one second, because it could not start: something already held the port it wanted, an export service I had started on Friday and never closed. I had put that step into the gate at 20:28, eight minutes before the run, precisely because nothing had been watching it. Its first unattended run discovered it could not run at all in the situation it was most likely to meet.

Here is the version of this that scares me. It reported red. A step that cannot start is one catch away from reporting green, and I have written that catch before. If compare had swallowed its own crash, it would have printed a tick in the same column, the same green, the same width as the fifteen that meant something. A green tick is a date, not a fact. It took until that night to learn that a green tick is also a question, and the only thing that makes it an answer is knowing what it did not ask.

Monday: the comparison with no true answer

Six items on the release checklist were red, and their reason read:

`` the last recorded gate run was at d00ed98, and HEAD is d00ed98: it proves nothing about this code ``

Those are the same commit. One side was seven characters, the other forty, and a plain !== between them is true always. So six lines could not be ticked by any run, at any commit, ever. It survived a week because whenever the gate's commit legitimately differed from HEAD — which is almost always — the broken comparison and a correct one give the same answer. It could only be seen on a day when the gate ran twice at one commit, and I only did that because the first run had failed on a port.

The same morning: the checklist's heading said 48 of 58 and the bar underneath said 38. The count came off the file, read before a single proof had run; the proofs then ticked and unticked items, and nobody counted again. The table always felt alive and the checklist always felt broken, and I had put that down to a feeling. It was not a feeling. The list itself, over those days, grew to 64 lines while I trusted it and shrank to 51 once each line had to prove itself.

And one I could not fix: any commit retired the gate's whole four-hour record, including a commit that fixed a typo in the README. The answer was already in the codebase, one layer down — the per-model checks die when a file on their own render path changes, not when anything changes — and I did not build it, because it changes what green means, and that is not a thing to change at five in the morning on the day you are trying to ship.

Act three: the three days the ticks stopped lying

Tuesday: the record that dies by its own files

Each of the gate's eighteen steps got the list of files it depends on, and I measured it the only way that proves anything: append a byte to a file, read the board again, see which steps go stale. A README edit retires nothing now. Three things fell out of writing the lists down. Twelve of the eighteen steps had never declared anything, so the board could never call them current — "not known", honest and useless. The build had been hashing its own output: 265 files, 141 of them files the build writes, so "the build is clean" would have gone stale the instant it was earned, for ever. And a rule change is not a code change: if the list itself is redefined, the old hash and the new are not comparable, and saying "stale" would be asserting that code moved when it may not have.

The afternoon found two eaten backslashes. The pattern that recognises the Worker's "out of exports for today" was meant to read HTTP 4\d\d; it read HTTP 4dd, which nothing has ever said, so every refusal fell through the guard built for it, a good measurement was overwritten with a failure, and the board reported that the two renderers disagreed — about pixels one of them had declined to draw. A false red is worth exactly what a false green is worth.

Then the evening. I deleted my working copy, cloned the repository, and ran everything the way a stranger would: open the board, press Run, wait. Three hours and fifty-seven minutes, 135 of 135. The first thing a clone showed me was that the board's own page in the repository was ten commits behind its source. And the second thing was a padlock. The checklist had worn one for a week, with the words "push is blocked until these hold" — and twice that day I had pushed to main with twenty-four items open, and each push had deployed. The checklist, the proofs and the sign-off all described a gate, and every one of them was a page or a printout.

Now two things say no. A git hook rebuilds the ledger and refuses the push unless stage one is complete and a person has said to push this commit. And because git lets the pushing machine skip its own hook, the deploy checks the same thing first. All three — the page, the hook, the deploy — read one verdict, written once. By midnight nobody ticks the list, except to say push.

Wednesday: eight models, and a still that is two pictures

The real test of all of this is not me; it is somebody who has never seen the repository adding a model. So eight agents each wrote one — one in each group, each in files of its own — and six of the eight lost their first quarter of an hour to the same thing: the dev server answered an unknown model with the home page, a 200 for the wrong page, and the check reported "nothing drawn", which points at the model. Fixed. All eight passed every check. Two were wrong anyway: the cradle's balls swung the wrong way, and the knob hid its own tick marks. So there is a script now that photographs a model, because the checks still cannot tell you whether a thing looks right, and the person who can needs a picture.

Then three models failed the text check with the same reading: a word in white on lilac, 2.44:1, after a click — "Ship", on the back of a box, turned away, a word nobody could see. The DOM never moved: same rectangles, same transform, no animation listed. I traced it through the DOM for a long time before taking the pictures the check takes and looking at them.

Between the first shot and the second, Chrome had redrawn the front face's word one pixel lower, about half a second after the turn ended, with nothing animating. The check compared two different pictures and read the shifted edges as the hidden word's pixels. The fix is a definition: a still is two pictures that agree. The check shoots, measures, and shoots again; if the two differ, it was not a still. 143 of 143, and the three models were never wrong.

The same evening every snippet became its own file, fetched when its model is mounted — a model page from 304 KB to 144 KB gzipped — which broke npm run build for twenty minutes, because the page generator could not see the new plugin. The gate's own build caught it; the deploy would have.

Thursday: a build that was not the build

Twice that night I had taken a release to 51 of 51 by hand — the stale steps, the jobs, the snapshot, the sign-off, the push, the deploy, the build, the served site. The board knew every one of those and offered them one button at a time. Now it offers the sequence: Prepare the release runs what the push is waiting for and stops at the sign-off, which stays a person's; After the push waits for the deploy, builds the same way, and asks the served site six questions.

The first run of After the push came back red: the served home page and the local build differed beyond the build stamp. It was right. A job started by the board inherits the board's environment, and the board builds the ledger through a development server, which leaves NODE_ENV=development set for the rest of the process's life. Every build that had ever run inside a board job had been a development build. The served site was the deploy's build; the local one was not. One commit later the second run said what the first could not: identical once the build stamp is set aside.

The number that would not arrive

The checklist is 51 lines: 45 that have to hold before a push, one that is mine, five that can only be answered after the deploy. So "done" has a number. For most of these days it read something else — 44, 42, 27 the evening I changed the frame every model is mounted in and four hours of results went stale at once, correctly. The first time it read 51 of 51 was Wednesday morning. I asked, in those words, whether we were done. The honest answer was: this commit is, and here are eight things that would make the next one better. By evening it was 27 again. It read 51 at a quarter past eleven, again at half past midnight, and the next morning I asked the same question and got three more.

That is where the frustration lived. Not in the red lines — a red line tells you what to do. In the green ones. 51 of 51 kept arriving and kept not meaning finished. I had thought, at the start, that the ledger would take three days. It has been almost two weeks.

What I eventually understood is that the number was never going to mean that. 51 of 51 is a green tick one size up: it says that this commit has been proven, by every proof there is, as of now. It says nothing about whether there should be a next commit. That question has no proof. It is the one thing on the list the ledger never answers, which is why the one line it cannot tick is the one that says a person decided to push.

Afterwards

What it cost

Two bills. The subscription: over the first six days, about 9.6 billion tokens across 32,800 replies — almost none of it new writing, nearly all of it the same conversation being read again on every turn, by everyone in it. The weekly allowance was 95% spent by the second evening. The three days after cost 2.2 billion across 4,900 replies, a quarter of that, with the fresh-clone gate, the push guard, eight models, the snippet split and twenty deploys in them.

What changed was not the work. It was who was talking, and when: the long runs ran unattended and reported when they ended; the questions got fewer and bigger; the two releases on the last night ran on one instruction — if something fails, fix it and continue until 51 of 51 — and I went to sleep.

The other bill is the laptop: an ASUS TUF A14 that is a gaming laptop and spent twelve days as a render farm. Twelve agents in an afternoon, fifty-seven browsers one evening, and four-hour runs that hold sixteen gigabytes down to three or four free. I could not play anything all fortnight. On the last morning it was hot again and I asked why, as if it were still checking something. It was my own browser tabs.

What this one taught me

Of everything I have built with AI, this is the most involved, and the one I learned the most from — not about CSS, about working with AI.

The hard part was never getting code written. It was getting what I could see in my head into something the model could see. Sunday's eleven hours were a picture not surviving the trip; Monday's twelve agents were what happens once the picture has become a rule on a page, because a rule survives. What changed in the second week is that the model used the app — cloned it, opened the board, pressed Run, read what the page said, the way a stranger would. The clone ten commits behind its source, the eight agents' missing page, a first page of cards that took six seconds to appear: none of that is visible from the code, all of it was visible from using the app — and once it had, once it knew why the ledger existed, it could say where it should go next.

So the lesson is not "describe it better". It is: give it the app, and the reason for the app. Then the imagination does not have to be translated. It can be checked.

Six things to tell someone starting

  • Write the rule down before the agents start. One page saying what "correct" means turns twelve agents from twelve arguments into an afternoon's work.
  • Make every record say what it judged. Not when, not at which commit — which files. Then a README fix costs a README fix, and a four-hour run is retired only by the things it was about.
  • "Didn't run" must never look like "passed." They are opposite facts, and a false red costs exactly what a false green costs — in the other direction, and sooner.
  • Break a check on purpose and watch it fail. A check you have never seen fail is decoration; the lettering check could never fail, because the thing it guarded against said OK.
  • If the page says the push is blocked, something has to block it. The reading that refuses the push must be the reading the page shows, and there must be two of them.
  • Keep your own eyes in it, and give them a picture. A ring that didn't wrap its planet, a cradle swinging the wrong way and a word redrawn a pixel lower all passed every check that existed. I found them by looking.
