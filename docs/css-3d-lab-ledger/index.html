Last week I had several AI agents working on the same codebase at once. They were fast. Then someone asked me whether the work was done, and I realised I could not answer without taking an agent's word for it.

So I spent four days building the thing that could: a ledger. 135 models, eleven checks, one page that says which of them hold right now and which went stale.

Four rules did most of the work.

→ A count has to balance. Every model sits in exactly one stage, the parts of a stage add up to the stage, and the build records whether it balanced. "It is like an accountant" is the note I sent, and it stuck.

→ A result goes stale when what it judged changes. Not on every commit — the check fingerprints what it actually looked at, including shared code and its own rule version. A pass under rule v1 never counts as a pass under v2.

→ "Didn't run" is never "passed". A crashed test gets its own bucket, named, and it blocks.

→ A check nobody has seen fail is decoration. Every check ships with a deliberately broken copy it must fail.

What that caught, in four days: 35 models with text below WCAG AA on one stage; 18 that only looked right because the site's stylesheet set box-sizing for them; MP4 exports coming out dark because the video stream carried no colour description; a click that piled up elements until the page froze; a zoom feature measuring 926 element boxes per keystroke.

What it could not catch: a planet's ring that didn't wrap the planet, a sun spilling below the horizon, a rocket that read as shrinking instead of launching. All three passed every check that existed. I found them by looking.

Cost, honestly: about 7.3 billion tokens across the agents, a weekly allowance gone by Tuesday, two collisions where two agents edited one file and one of them lost, a gate that once had 57 headless browsers open and made me restart the laptop, and background waiters left running for 25 hours.

If you are running agents in parallel and the building is fast but nobody can tell you the state, feel free to DM me. I am open to consulting and product/engineering advisory work.

The whole process, mistakes included, is in the article below.

The ledger: four days of AI agents on one codebase

Several Claude agents working on 135 models at once, and the one page that had to answer "is it done?" without taking anyone's word for it. Counted from git, the checks and the session logs.

Edgaras Neverdauskas19 min read4,339 words

Projectcss3dlab.edgarasneverdauskas.com ↗

Where it stood

CSS 3D Lab was built in about a day, and that day has its own article. What follows is the four days after it, which took longer than the day that made it.

The gallery was finished and it was not consistent. A model had to look and behave identically on a gallery card, in the viewer, on its own page, in the editor, on the large stage, at full screen, and in the file a recording or a snapshot hands you. It didn't. The app measured each model and nudged it into place from outside, so every one of those surfaces was a fresh chance to get it wrong. On Monday morning I sent a run of screenshots of cards that did not match and wrote: "this is also not constant size, what the hell man." Then: "yes, you need to focus on redesigning the models rather than some hacks around."

By that afternoon several Claude agents were rewriting models in parallel, other agents were reviewing their work, and I had lost the ability to answer one question. I asked it at 14:42: "ok, so where can I see the checkmark UI, app side, what is happening: like what was already checked, approved, what is in progress and is in queue." Nobody could tell me without me believing them. That is the whole story. Everything below is what it took to be able to answer it.

Since Sunday there have been 613 commits, and none of them are pushed yet. As with J.A.R.V.I.S. and Self-Aware Writing, the numbers here come from the git history, the check records and the session logs, not from memory. My own messages are quoted with the typos fixed; the wording is mine.

The contract: outcomes, not designs

The rewrite started at 10:23 on Monday with a file, docs/VIEW-CONTRACT.md, and its first line is the decision: "Nothing outside a model adjusts it: there is no per-model size or offset anywhere in the app, no measuring run and no override file. A model that looks wrong is a model whose own code is wrong."

Three commits in the next thirty-four minutes carried that out. "Hold a model's height, not its shape, and drop the second implementation" at 10:31. "Retire the measuring run" at 10:39. "The frame is the canvas: take the placement machinery out" at 10:57. Then, at 11:01, the first check: "Add the check that judges models instead of adjusting them."

The contract fixes outcomes a visitor can measure, and each one names the check that measures it: centred within 4vmin of the middle both ways; inside a size band of 40 to 70vmin tall and at most 92% wide; text readable against the pixels behind it on the dark stage and the light one; finished at rest; the same on every surface; no reliance on the page's CSS around it. How a model gets there is free, in writing: "Claude, who writes the models, must stay free to invent new ones."

That freedom was not generosity, it was a repair. The first rules bent good designs until they broke. A flip clock was restacked onto two lines to reach a height floor. A dropdown was grown to 40vmin while closed, which no dropdown does. The contract now has a heading for it — when a good design does not fit, add or adjust a class; do not bend the model — and three shape classes a model can declare: wide (a width floor instead of a height floor: lit, waveletters and clock use it), expands (no floor at rest, but its open state must reach 40vmin: dropdown and radial), and full-canvas, for a scene that covers the whole canvas by design. A class costs five things in the same change: the mark in the model's entry, the rule written down with its reason, the check taught to read the mark, a deliberately broken copy the check fails, and the docs updated. It is never inferred, so every exception is visible.

All 135 models were rewritten to that contract on Monday between 11:25 and 15:50, in 133 commits.

Monday: 133 commits in four hours

The work was split into lettered batches. Convert model batch A at 11:35, batch B a minute later, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J, K, L — twelve batches of models, each its own agent, plus agents on the control zone, the export dialog's slider, cross-stage QA and the ruler the checks measure with. Seventy helper agents started that day. The 15:00 hour alone holds 100 commits.

It is not a thing a laptop enjoys. At 14:28: "maybe too many agents are running in parallel, my laptop started to lag and heat too much, let's reduce the number a bit." At 19:45, again: "seems like we are running a lot of agents and the pc is lagging, maybe we can reduce a bit." We settled on a number — "it can be up to 5, I think laptops can handle that many agents" — and mostly kept to it.

The reviewing came from one message at 11:35: "yeah, so every time you change something go check visually; also maybe have another agent do a second QA across all stages." That became the shape of the whole week: an agent fixes, a different agent looks. By Monday evening I liked it enough to say so: "I also like this experiment with working with you separately and agents also doing their job in parallel and checking each other and reporting to you for your final approval."

Two days later the same arrangement cost me an evening. More on that below.

"It is like an accountant": why the ledger appeared

The first ledger was committed at 14:52 on Monday — ten minutes after I asked where I could see the state. By 15:25 it was live: a watcher that rebuilds it whenever a model file, a check result, a review or the commit changes, and a small queue file the lead session writes what it is working on into.

At 16:47 I gave it its rule: "across all the converted, checked, approved, the numbers must match — it is like an accountant, you know." That sentence is now load-bearing. The ledger's counts balance: every model is in exactly one of three stages, the parts of each stage sum to the stage, and the build records whether it balanced. When it doesn't, it says so rather than rounding.

Three more rules came out of the same week.

A result goes stale when what it judged changes. At 08:22 on Tuesday: "also note, when we make edits to the models they have to go through the checking process again." The naive version of that makes everything stale on every commit, which is useless. What it does instead is fingerprint what each check actually judged — the resolved snippet with shared constants filled in, how the model is played with, its text, and the files on that check's render path — so a contract result does not go stale when only the model's description changes, and a text review does not go stale when the model frame changes. It names what went stale and at which commit.

A rule's own version counts. Every check carries a ruleVersion in one registry file, bumped whenever the meaning of the rule changes: a limit made stricter, a new thing judged, a surface added. A result records the version it was judged under, and a pass under version 1 never counts as a pass under version 2. The contract check is on rule 3, the export check on rule 7, the contrast check on rule 2. The export check's rule 7 is a paragraph about hairlines, written the day it was needed.

"Didn't run" is never "passed." A check that crashed, timed out, or was never run on a model is its own bucket, named in the breakdown, and it blocks. The run records are read line by line as they arrive, so a crash halfway through keeps every verdict printed before it — and loses none of them to silence.

The checks, one for each new kind of bug

There are eleven checks now, nine that gate a model and two that judge the site. They did not arrive as a plan. Each one appeared the day something got through.

Contract (Monday 11:01). Same on every surface (12:39). Motion, a filmstrip of every loop, hover, control and focus (14:50). The verify gate and the export check (15:37). Then SEO, share previews and pause-and-access within half an hour of each other on Tuesday morning at 03:15, 03:19 and 03:42. Box sizing at 11:26 and text contrast at 11:39 on Tuesday. Performance on Wednesday evening at 21:03, and the whole-gallery check six minutes later.

A check is only trusted once it fails a deliberately broken copy. The contrast check ships with four: the real radio model (passes, lowest 4.87:1), its caption forced to a fixed grey (fails at 2.49:1 on the light stage), its pills white on a light tint (fails at 1.46:1), and one with a disabled, faded control (passes, and prints "1 disabled text exempt"). The box-sizing check has six. The performance check is proved on a leaky copy that adds 900 elements over thirty clicks and a heavy one that draws 901 and takes 258ms a frame; both fail, each naming the budget it broke. Every budget in it was set from what the other 134 models actually do, not guessed: at most 250 elements at rest, when the heaviest good model, stairs, draws 158.

What the checks caught that I never would have

The first run of each new check is where the cost of not having had it shows up.

Text. The contrast check's first pass over all 135 models: 100 passed. The other 35 had text that failed WCAG AA against the actual pixels behind the letters, on one stage or the other — the signature on a greeting card at 1.7:1 against the dark stage, white words on a bookshelf's teal and amber spines at 1.6:1. They were fixed in the models' own code over the next hour.

Box sizing. The first run: 117 of 135. Eighteen models drew differently with and without a page rule making every box border-box — because they had been written inside the site, whose stylesheet sets it, and their standalone copies had nothing setting it. The visible one I caught myself, on Tuesday at 08:41: "bookshelf has broken, like the cover of the book is higher than the book." The pulled-out book's front cover stood 16 units taller and wider than its spine and page block, hanging below the shelf. Every face now declares its own box sizing, and the eighteen that genuinely mean content-box say so with a reason the check prints.

Colour in MP4s. Exported videos came out slightly dark and green. Chromium's encoder converts the canvas to full-range BT.709 and writes that into the file's container, but its software H.264 encoder leaves the colour description out of the stream itself, so any decoder reading the stream guesses BT.601. The test pattern was 3.8 levels off on average and 25 at worst. The fix rewrites the sequence header to carry the colour the encoder reports, copying every other bit. After: 0.37 levels average, 2 at worst.

Confetti freezing the page. Each click made 36 pieces that only removed themselves when their animation ended — and a paused stage never ends one, so clicking fast piled up 3D layers until the browser stopped. The pieces are now made once and thrown again and again: at most 108 exist, and a click gives the next 36 new numbers. This became a check of its own, the pile-up budget, which does the same interaction thirty times and fails a model that keeps growing. No other model does it.

The view zoom that measured everything. The same confetti froze the model page and the export dialog while staying smooth on a gallery card, which made no sense until it did: the cards have no limit watcher and those stages do. Every animation start and end ran a whole look-ahead — every element in the scene read for what it paints, every running animation sought twelve times and measured — and one confetti click is 36 of those, then 36 more as the pieces land. Measured inside the frame, thirty fast clicks: the model's own work was 2.8ms a burst while the page's worst frame was 2.6 to 3.8 seconds. After: worst frame 83 to 133ms, median 8.3ms. Per CSS keystroke, confetti went from 926 element measurements to 151.

A badge showing through a wall. On Thursday morning the contrast check held package at 3.72:1 on a white "NEW" label. Filmed at rest, the box is shut and the badge is nowhere on screen. What the check had measured was 13 anti-aliased pixels, one over its threshold of 12.3: the card's plane losing its depth sort against the front wall and printing a ghost of the word through it. Where the badge is actually seen, it reads 5.81:1. The fix was the leak, not the colour.

What no check can judge

The contract says this in as many words: whether the 3D is correct, and whether the motion reads as the thing it is meant to be, is left to a person. Five hundred and fifty-nine review entries are recorded in docs/reviews/, 279 visual and 280 text, under nine reviewer names including a final independent pass over all 135. The visual reviews are close-up: the entry for phone reads "Phone stands centred at about 60vmin tall on card and editor canvas, clear of the corners; the paused pose is a clean three-quarter view." The text reviews compare a model's words against its own code, and one pass found twelve models describing something the code no longer did.

And then there is my eye, which found things both of them missed.

Tuesday 07:43: "also one of the planet rings is not wrapping the planet — I wonder if it's because the model was not yet visually checked by agent, or something else was missed?" It had been checked. The ring was one ellipse drawn over the ball with its top border hidden, so its far half vanished even where it should have shown beside the planet, and its sides crossed in front of it. It is now the same tilted ellipse drawn twice and cut along its long axis, the far half behind the ball and the near half in front.

Three minutes later: "also the synthwave seems broken." The sun was placed at 14% of the canvas height but sized in vmin, while the horizon sits at 50%, so where its striped bands reached depended on the canvas shape — and on a card two of them fell below the horizon and floated over the grid. It is anchored to the horizon now and is only the part above it.

Then the rocket, which the queue entry records in my words: "the user says these animations look weird now; they were fine before." The launch read as a shrinking rocket rather than a climbing one. It was flying 5,000 units into depth toward a vanishing point on its own shoulder, so the nose never rose: it just got smaller in place beside the gantry and grew again on the way back. The frame is a window onto the sky now, and the ground drops out of the bottom while the rocket leaves through the top.

And a small one, at 08:43 on Tuesday: "for unboxing would like the initial state of box be completely closed." No check has an opinion about whether a box should start shut.

Where two agents met

Twice, two agents worked on the same file at the same time and one of them lost.

The first was Wednesday at 18:30, and the recovery commit says it plainly: "My three commits before this one were spliced from the working tree against the wrong base: they carried my hunks but dropped the stage-arrow work that had landed in 1877c11, and the last of them picked up a few lines of that agent's next, uncommitted change." What got committed was the honest state — the other agent's tree with only the lead's own changes on top — and nothing was lost. Eleven minutes later another commit took the opposite precaution, in writing: "The strip belongs to another agent, so it is not edited here."

The second was two hours after that, and I watched it happen. 20:37: "can you explain what is happening with the ledger UI? It seems it gets updated and then somehow reverted to the old UI." 20:39: "now the table changes are old also — something is not in sync with our approach with agents, they should be aware of each other, and you should also." 20:46: "no, the ledger is not back to the old version, what is going on, we need to stop this." And then the rule, at 20:49: "make sure that two agents don't overlap each other when it comes to the design, this is important."

The release checklist now opens with a group called Nothing is lost, and one of its items exists only because of this: the main index must match HEAD, "so a plain git commit cannot undo commits made with commit-tree."

What we got wrong

  • Rules that bent good designs. A flip clock restacked onto two lines, a dropdown grown to full size while closed. "Outcomes, not designs" is the fix, and it had to be written after the damage.
  • A gate with no ceiling on browsers. Before the cap, it split its work by the cores and the free memory it found, and the render service opened a Chromium per request on top of that: one run reached 57 headless browsers and the machine had to be restarted. On Thursday morning I hit the same wall again — "I had to restart laptop, it was getting crazy, we need a better approach for checking the list" — and there is now one number for how many browsers may be open anywhere, default 2, read by the gate and the service alike. It is still in the working tree, uncommitted at the time of writing.
  • Idle waiters left running for 25 hours. Wednesday 18:14: "these are like running for 25 hours, man." Then, when they were cleared: "funny that they went and just gone, like quit the job without saying."
  • Reruns caused by our own changes. Every time a check's rule was tightened, every result recorded under the old rule went stale on purpose, and all 135 models had to be run again. That is the system working, and it is expensive.
  • The ledger's own stage strip, redesigned five times in a day. It was three glass tanks of liquid, then one sea under three skies with a whale and a dolphin crossing on coprime cycles, then flat colour under a single wave, then one continuous path whose height eases through the gates. I asked for the fish ("maybe put inside some kind of water animal like dolphin, shark and whale"), and I asked for them to go.
  • Approved was pink, then green, then pink again, over about an hour of me saying "why is approved a red colour? It should be green, no? Red seems danger or alert" and then "or actually it should be pink like the design."
  • A model page's weight budget that failed 136 pages at once, all on one rule, while the ledger's bar made it look like the embed pages were the problem. The budget was re-measured and moved up with the same headroom, and every group now prints its own pass count.

What it cost

The subscription, and the machine's patience.

From Sunday to Thursday the models and their agents used about 7.29 billion tokens. Almost all of that is cache reads of context read again on every turn: 7.10 billion read, 171 million written to cache, 13.9 million written out as actual replies, and 86 thousand of genuinely new input. That is 27,896 replies across the lead session and 112 helper agents. Monday alone was 3.0 billion tokens and 14,764 replies. Across the whole project, launch day included, the total is 9.62 billion.

The weekly allowance ran out on Tuesday. 08:32: "I am running out of the weekly limits and it is only Tuesday." 13:14: "we are already 93 percent limit reached this week, so keep that in mind." 18:14: "please don't use too much credit for this now, I am at 95% of weekly." 19:48: "I am at 96% of limit now." Tuesday ended on 143 commits against Monday's 391, and on Wednesday morning a one-off reset arrived: "we are back in business, my friend — there was one free reset per month."

The laptop paid the rest. A full verify run opens browsers for hours, so the gate is written for an idle machine and says so at the top of the file. At one point I asked whether it could run while I played GTA 5. It could not. I turned the game off.

There were crashes on both sides. The checks now relaunch a broken browser and run the model once more, because one failed screenshot used to poison every model after it in the same process. Claude Code itself crashed twice that I recorded: "same as with the table where the header hides — also you crashed, so I restarted Claude Code", and, at four in the morning, "seems like you crashed, my friend."

Where it ended, and what is still open

The release snapshot taken at the current commit says 135 of 135 approved: every one of the nine per-model checks a fresh pass on the code as it stands, plus a fresh visual and a fresh text review, neither of them reporting a problem. The motion check passes 108 outright; the other 27 raised flags it cannot rule on alone, and each flag has a named person's ruling with a reason — the last seven were ruled by eye on Thursday morning. The raw run records are deliberately not in the repository, so anyone who clones it rebuilds the ledger from their own runs rather than trusting mine.

The lead's queue holds 88 pieces of work: 85 done, two queued, one running. The two still queued are the full verify gate over all 135 models and an export verifier fan-out over every setting the dialog offers. The release checklist has 53 items; 14 of their proofs run locally and hold, 9 currently fail, and 30 need a real run, the network or a person. The failing ones are honest about themselves: the working tree is not clean, the queue has something running, and the built site still carries a development fallback address.

Nothing is pushed. 613 commits sit on the local branch.

What is reusable is not the code. It is four sentences: a count must balance; a result must go stale when what it judged changes, including the rule itself; "didn't run" must never read as "passed"; and a check nobody has seen fail is not a check. The rest — eleven checks, a contract, a queue and a page that draws them — is just what those four sentences look like once you have 135 of something and more than one worker.

What I would tell someone starting

  • Make the thing satisfy the rule, not the machinery around it. Removing the measuring run cost a day and ended a whole class of bug.
  • Ask where you can see the state, early. The ledger existed ten minutes after the question and changed how the rest of the week was run.
  • Write the rule down before the agents run. Every batch was told to read the contract first, so a decision made once stayed made.
  • A check you have never seen fail is decoration. Break a copy on purpose and make it fail.
  • Set budgets from measurements, not opinions. Every performance budget came from running all 135 first.
  • Give two agents two files. Where that is impossible, make one of them refuse to touch the other's, and say so in the commit.
  • Let the rule bend before the design does. A rule that makes a good thing worse is a missing exception, not a bad design.
  • Keep your own eye in the loop. The ring that didn't wrap the planet, the sun below the horizon, the rocket that shrank — all three passed every check that existed.

In one paragraph

Four days, 613 commits, 135 models, 112 helper agents and eleven checks, spent almost entirely on making one gallery behave the same everywhere and on being able to prove it. The contract said what a model must satisfy and left how free. The ledger counted, went stale honestly, and refused to read a crash as a pass. The checks caught a video's missing colour, a page-freezing click and a bookshelf whose cover hung below its shelf. I caught a ring that didn't wrap its planet and a rocket that shrank instead of launching. The agents made the work fast; the ledger made it finishable; and the last word on whether a thing looks right is still a person looking at it.

The lab is at css3dlab.edgarasneverdauskas.com and the code is open source at github.com/Evirtual/css-3d-lab, where docs/VIEW-CONTRACT.md, docs/RELEASE-CHECKLIST.md and scripts/checks-registry.mjs are the three files this article is really about. Built with Claude Code and Claude Opus 5.
