Last week I had several AI agents working on the same codebase at once. They were fast. Then someone asked me whether the work was done, and I realised I could not answer without taking an agent's word for it.

I couldn't check 135 models by hand, so I spent six days building the thing that could: a ledger. Every model has to clear it before approval, and the last check, the visual one, is mine. Eleven checks, one page that says which of them hold right now and which went stale.

Four rules did most of the work.

→ A count has to balance. Every model sits in exactly one stage, the parts of a stage add up to the stage, and the build records whether it balanced. "It is like an accountant" is the note I sent, and it stuck.

→ A result goes stale when what it judged changes. Not on every commit — the check fingerprints what it actually looked at, including shared code and its own rule version. A pass under rule v1 never counts as a pass under v2.

→ "Didn't run" is never "passed". A crashed test gets its own bucket, named, and it blocks.

→ A check nobody has seen fail is decoration. Every check ships with a deliberately broken copy it must fail.

What that caught, in six days: 35 models with text below WCAG AA on one stage; 18 that only looked right because the site's stylesheet set box-sizing for them; MP4 exports coming out dark because the video stream carried no colour description; a click that piled up elements until the page froze; a zoom feature measuring 926 element boxes per keystroke.

What it could not catch: a planet's ring that didn't wrap the planet, a sun spilling below the horizon, a rocket that read as shrinking instead of launching. All three passed every check that existed. I found them by looking.

Cost, honestly: about 7.3 billion tokens across the agents, a weekly allowance gone by Tuesday, two collisions where two agents edited one file and one of them lost, a gate that once had 57 headless browsers open and made me restart the laptop, and background waiters left running for 25 hours.

If you are running agents in parallel and the building is fast but nobody can tell you the state, feel free to DM me. I am open to consulting and product/engineering advisory work.

The whole process, mistakes included, is in the article below.

135 CSS 3D models, and I couldn't check the technical side of each by hand. So: eleven automated checks every model must clear before approval, and the last check, the visual one, is mine.

Six days of AI agents, and what I learned about the canvas: https://articles.edgarasneverdauskas.com/css-3d-lab-ledger/

Six days · 694 commits

A green tick is a date, not a fact

The Ledger behind CSS 3D Lab: eleven automated checks each of its 135 models has to pass, because I couldn’t check the technical side by hand, and then my own visual review. Six days of Claude agents, and what I learned about the canvas on the way. Counted from git, the checks and the session logs.

Edgaras Neverdauskas21 min read4,913 words

Projectcss3dlab.edgarasneverdauskas.com ↗

It started with wanting to export a picture

CSS 3D Lab is a gallery of 135 three-dimensional models: a rotating cube, a flip clock, a bookshelf you can turn. There is no WebGL and no 3D library. Each model is ordinary HTML, and the depth comes from CSS transforms. 93 of them are HTML and CSS alone; in the other 42, a little JavaScript feeds numbers in and CSS still does all the drawing.

I built it with Claude in about a day, and it was fine. That day has its own article.

Then I wanted one more feature: let people export a model — as a video, as an image, as a printable sheet. That is the whole reason for everything that follows. Six days, a rulebook, eleven automated checks, 1,215 separate verdicts and a page built to hold all of it, because I wanted a download button.

Here is why that one feature costs so much.

To put a model in a file, something has to decide what the model should look like. And the moment I looked, no two places agreed.

The bookshelf is the clearest example. In the small gallery card it looked right. Opened full screen, one book's cover hung off the edge of the shelf. Exported as a picture, the cover moved again. One model, three places, three slightly different bookshelves.

Nearly every model had some version of it, most too small to complain about. A few pixels. A slight drift. Nobody files a bug about a few pixels, which is exactly what makes them expensive — and a picture is a receipt. Once somebody downloads it, the drift is permanent and it has their name on it.

So the week's job became: make 135 models look the same everywhere, and be able to prove it.

The proving turned out to be the hard part.

Sunday: eleven hours on one panel

The first day went almost entirely on the panel where you choose a shape and a size and take a picture of a model.

Eleven hours. One panel. Message by message, me describing what was wrong and Claude fixing it.

That day cost 1.61 billion tokens, and only 238 million of them were an agent doing work. The rest was conversation — me explaining, it replying, and both of us re-reading the whole discussion on every turn. I did not know that at the time; you only see it later, in the logs.

Monday 10:23: somebody writes the rule down

Monday started by taking out the fixes that had been made by hand — a nudge here to stop something sticking out there. A model held together by nudges cannot be checked, because there is no rule it is supposed to follow.

So at 10:23 the rule went into a file. One page saying what every model must do: size itself against the space it is given rather than to a fixed number, never be too small or spill over its edges, stay centred, and look the same in a card, in the editor, full screen and in a picture.

Then things started coming out of the project, fast.

  • Monday morning
  • 10:23the rule is written down
  • 10:31hold a model's height, not its shape — and delete the second way of doing it
  • 10:39retire the measuring run entirely
  • 10:57the frame is the canvas: take the placement machinery out
  • 11:01add the check that judges models instead of quietly adjusting them

That last line is the whole week in one commit. Until then, code had been correcting models on their way to the screen. After it, nothing corrected anything — a model either satisfied the rule or it was wrong, and something said so.

By 11:31 the commits had turned into a list of names: write dice in canvas units. Write the door in canvas units. One model at a time.

At 11:35 I added the thing I would repeat all week: "yeah, so every time you change something go check visually; also maybe have another agent do a second QA."

Monday afternoon: twelve agents, and a hot laptop

In the afternoon the rule got handed to twelve agents at once.

They rewrote every model in the gallery against it. Twelve Claude sessions, each on its own files, each having read the same page first. Four and a half hours, 253 commits. The 15:00 hour alone holds 100 of them, the busiest hour of the entire week by some distance.

It cost about a billion tokens — roughly what Sunday had cost. Sunday produced one panel. This produced the gallery.

At 14:28 the machine started complaining: "maybe too many agents are running in parallel, my laptop started to lag and heat too much, let's reduce the number." At 19:45, again: "seems like we are running a lot of agents and the pc is lagging."

That is the part nobody mentions about running twelve agents. They are not free, and the thing they cost is the computer you are sitting at.

Monday 14:42: "where can I see what is happening?"

By mid-afternoon I had a problem that had nothing to do with code.

Twelve agents were finishing work. Each one told me it was done. I had no way of knowing whether any of them were right, and no way to see all of it at once. I was reading commit messages and taking their word for it.

So at 14:42 I asked where I could see what was happening.

At 15:25 it was live: one page, every model a row, every check a column, rebuilding itself as results arrived.

One rule sits behind it, and everything else follows from that one rule.

A result expires when the thing it judged changes.

Change a model's code and every verdict about that model goes grey and has to be earned again. Nobody can approve something into being finished. You cannot argue with it, and neither can I.

Tuesday: the day the money ran out

Tuesday was the quietest day and possibly the most useful: more lines came out of the project than went into it. Some of that was the last of the hand-made fixes. Most of it was work the page had made pointless — status notes, progress comments, little scripts that only existed to answer questions the page now answered by itself.

It was also the day the subscription ran out.

08:32 — "I am running out of the weekly limits and it is only Tuesday." 13:14 — "we are already 93 percent limit reached this week." 18:14 — "please don't use too much credit for this now, I am at 95% of weekly." 19:48 — "I am at 96% of limit now."

The 18:14 one is my favourite, and not because it is dramatic. It is tacked onto the end of a message about where a panel should sit. Worry about the bill and one more design change, in the same breath.

Tuesday ended on 143 commits. Monday had ended on 391. There was not less to do. I was counting every message.

Wednesday 20:37: two agents, one file

Twice that week, two agents worked on the same file at once, and one of them lost.

The first was Wednesday at 18:30, and the recovery commit does not soften it: "My three commits before this one were spliced from the working tree against the wrong base: they carried my hunks but dropped the stage-arrow work that had landed in 1877c11." Eleven minutes later another commit took the opposite precaution and said so out loud: "The strip belongs to another agent, so it is not edited here."

The second one I watched happen live.

20:37 — "can you explain what is happening with the ledger UI? It seems it gets updated and then somehow reverted to the old UI." 20:39 — "now the table changes are old also — something is not in sync with our approach with agents, they should be aware of each other, and you should also." 20:46 — "no, the ledger is not back to the old version, what is going on, we need to stop this."

That is what the failure actually looks like. Not a crash. Not a warning. Two agents each make a sensible change, each reports success, and the second quietly writes over the first. Both of them told me the truth. The work was gone anyway.

The rule came at 20:49: "make sure that two agents don't overlap each other when it comes to the design, this is important."

The fix is not clever coordination. It is giving each agent its own files and, where that is impossible, telling one of them in writing not to touch the other's. The release checklist now opens with a group called Nothing is lost, and one of its items exists only because of that evening.

Thursday: the night the checks ran

By Thursday the agents had mostly run out of things to do, and the work had moved to the machine.

Checking a model means opening a browser, loading it, measuring it and closing it again. For 135 models and eleven checks, that is a great many browsers. At one point a run opened 57 at once and the laptop had to be restarted. Twice.

The fix was a limit of two browsers at a time. It turns a run that took minutes into one that takes hours, and it is the difference between a check you can leave going overnight and a check that takes your computer away from you.

That night it ran unattended for hours and cost 214 million tokens — about a quarter of what one evening of moving a progress bar around had cost earlier in the week.

What I was actually fighting all week

Not the code. I want to be accurate about this, because it surprised me.

Almost none of the six days was spent on CSS, or on models, or on anything you could call the product. It went on the machinery underneath: memory, the operating system, browsers, processes that were running locally, processes that were running somewhere else, a rendering service with its own rules, and the limits of the AI models themselves.

The laptop has sixteen gigabytes. A full run of the checks would take it down to under two, and twice it had to be restarted. Twelve agents at once made it lag and heat up enough that I asked for fewer. The browsers that do the checking open and close 135 times per check, and there are eleven checks.

Then the operating system had opinions. One watchdog looked for the running program and never found it, because on Windows it was looking in the wrong kind of process list entirely. Another knocked on the wrong door and declared the server dead — the address it asked for and the address the server answers on were not the same address, for reasons specific to Windows.

Then the remote half. The service that draws exported pictures runs on somebody else's computer, with its own limits on how often it will start a browser and how many minutes a day it will give you. Finding that out cost me a day's allowance in a single afternoon of testing.

And then the AI limits, which are their own weather system: the weekly allowance running dry on a Tuesday, agents stopping mid-task, and the constant arithmetic of what a question is worth asking.

The product was never the hard part. The hard part was every layer between the product and the picture of it.

Friday 09:24: the gate holds

On the fifth morning the full run printed the line the whole week had been aimed at:

GATE HOLDS: every model held every check

Nine checks judge each model. Two more judge the site as a whole. Between them they produce 1,215 separate verdicts, and the page shows every one.

A check that crashed is not a pass and it is not a failure. It is named, counted separately, and it blocks. That sounds like a small distinction and it is the most important thing on the page: "didn't run" must never look like "passed".

Some of what it caught I would have shipped without blinking. A ring of text that didn't quite wrap around its planet. A sun sitting below its own horizon. A rocket that shrank slightly as it flew. And the bookshelf, whose books were fine in a card and wrong full screen.

The branch went up at 19:47: 694 commits, built somewhere other than my laptop for the first time, green in four minutes and forty-two seconds.

Friday night: the card with its numbers hanging off

Then somebody opened the export panel and, in about ten minutes, found something that 135 models and eleven checks had not.

There is a model of a payment card. Take a picture of it on my laptop and the long card number sits neatly inside the edge. Take the same picture from the live site — same model, same settings — and the last digits hang off the end.

It is the bookshelf again, wearing a different hat.

When you take a picture, your browser doesn't draw it. It sends a description of the model to another computer, which draws it properly and sends the image back. That other computer didn't have the same lettering. It used whatever it did have, which was very slightly wider — wide enough, across sixteen digits, to push the last ones off the card.

Every check had passed, because every check had only ever asked my machine to draw the picture. Nothing had ever asked the other one the same question.

That is not a check that failed. It is a check nobody wrote, which is worse, because a gap makes no noise.

Friday night: sixteen fixes, and no gate at all

Here is the part I am least comfortable writing down.

Between 19:08 and 23:29 that Friday, sixteen commits went straight to the live site. Not one of them went through the gate.

The gate takes two and a half hours. The site was broken in front of somebody who was using it. So I did what anybody does: fixed the thing, checked the one thing I had just changed, and pushed.

At 22:17 I pushed a change to how the export panel arranges itself. At 22:19 I reverted it.

Two minutes. That is precisely the class of mistake the gate exists to catch, and the gate was not consulted — because consulting it would have taken until half past midnight, with the site still wrong the whole time.

I do not have a clean answer to this. A proof that takes two and a half hours is not a proof when something is wrong right now. It is a delay, and people route around delays. The first thing that happened when I routed around mine was that I shipped something I had to take back within two minutes.

The honest version is that I built a careful process for the calm part of the week and then abandoned it for the part where it would have mattered most. The whole point was not taking anybody's word for it — and on Friday night, the person taking things on trust was me.

On Saturday morning I went back to it. Five more fixes, committed and deliberately not pushed, while the whole run goes again from the beginning. It started at 10:28 and will take until the middle of the afternoon.

What I actually needed on Friday night did not exist: something between "trust yourself" and "wait two and a half hours". A way to ask one question about one model and get an answer in a minute. The gate can only answer everything at once, which means that when you are in a hurry, it answers nothing.

Saturday morning: the lettering that was never there

So we started sending the lettering along with the model. Two nights later the numbers still hung off, and what was actually happening is the funniest thing I found all week.

The lettering lives in one folder at the top of the site. But the code asked for it from wherever you happened to be standing — and you are always standing on some model's own page. So it went looking for lettering inside the bookshelf's folder. There has never been any lettering in there.

On the live site that request simply fails, and nothing gets sent at all.

On my own machine it was worse. Ask my development server for a file that doesn't exist and it politely hands back the website's home page instead — while saying "found it". The code had a check for exactly this: if the answer isn't OK, stop. The answer was OK. So the home page got packed up and sent as the lettering.

Lettering made out of a web page doesn't work. Nothing complains. The picture is simply drawn in whatever the other computer had lying around.

A week of "the exported picture doesn't match", a whole system built to send the lettering, eighty lines of styling rewritten — and none of it had ever arrived.

The part I keep thinking about is that check. If the answer isn't OK, stop is a real check. It is the check you would write. And it could never once have caught this, because the thing it guards against says OK.

Saturday: a pass recorded three days earlier

The quietest finding came last, and it is the one I would put on a wall.

A check failed on a model. I went looking for what had broken it, and found that the model's previous result said pass — recorded three days earlier.

Nothing had broken it. It had been failing for three days. The board was green because nothing had asked the question again since Wednesday, and a tick sitting in a file has no idea what day it is.

The expiry rule works: change something, and its verdicts go grey. But it only fires when something changes. A model nobody touches keeps its tick for ever, and for ever is a good deal longer than a tick stays true.

The page now fades a tick as it ages — full colour the day it ran, almost grey a fortnight later. It doesn't expire it and it doesn't fail it. A board that turned red every time you went on holiday would only teach you to ignore red.

It took about fifteen lines. The date had been sitting in the file the whole time.

Saturday afternoon: the check that covered six per cent of its name

While the full run was going, I glanced at the page and saw something that looked wrong. The export row said most of its models had "passed on older code". Everything else was green and current. I assumed the page was confused, because a moment earlier the export step had reported no problems at all.

The page was not confused. I was.

There are two ways the exports get checked. One goes over all 135 models at their default settings. The other takes a sample of eight and tries every combination the panel offers. The overnight run was supposed to do the first.

It was doing the second. The command needs to be handed the list of models, and it was not being handed anything, so it quietly fell back to its eight-model sample — and reported that as the export step.

So 127 of the 135 export verdicts were from two days earlier, before every font change of the week. Exports are the exact thing those font changes affect. The step that should have proved the fix had been checking six per cent of what its name implies, and passing.

And the page had been saying so the whole time, in a colour meaning "this was true about older code". I looked straight at it and decided the page was wrong.

There was a second tell I had already walked past. In the run's own status file, every step writes a sentence about what it found — except that one, which wrote a blank. I saw the blank at ten to one that afternoon, called it cosmetic, and moved on. It was not cosmetic. It was the step saying it had very little to report, because it had barely done anything.

That is the eighth of these I found in three days, and the most expensive. Not because it hid a bug, but because it made a claim I was leaning on. Every time I said the font problem was fixed, I was standing on eight models and calling it a hundred and thirty-five.

What it cost

Two bills. One was the subscription, one was the laptop.

Over six days we used about 8.8 billion tokens across 31,327 replies. That number is less impressive than it sounds: almost none of it is new writing. It is the same conversation being read again on every turn, by everyone in it.

Where it went is the interesting part. Sunday, one panel, talked through message by message: 1.61 billion. Monday, twelve agents rewriting all 135 models: about a billion, and most of that was agents working rather than me describing. One evening moving a progress bar around: 776 million.

Talking is the expensive part. Deciding how something looks is the most expensive thing of all. And the cheapest work in the entire week was the night nobody was watching.

Why there were no agents left by the end

The clearest number in the week is the one that goes to zero.

  • 7agents on Sunday
  • 53on Monday
  • 32on Tuesday
  • 15on Wednesday
  • 5on Thursday
  • 0on Friday

Nothing was taken away and no decision was made to stop using them. The work they were doing kept getting written down instead — first as the rule about what a model must do, then as checks that could answer it without being asked nicely, then as a list where every item named its own proof.

Each of those replaced a conversation. By Friday there was nothing left that needed one.

The agents were never the destination. They were how you get from "I know what I want" to "the machine knows what I want", quickly.

So is the thing any good?

The honest answer has three parts and they do not entirely agree with each other.

The checks found things I never would have. A ring of text that did not quite wrap around its planet. A sun below its own horizon. A rocket that shrank as it flew. The bookshelf. I looked at those models dozens of times and saw nothing, because after the fortieth look you stop seeing.

And the checks did not find the ones that mattered most. The exported picture not matching the preview, and the lettering that was never being sent — both found by somebody clicking a button on the live site, minutes after it went up. Eleven checks, 135 models, 1,215 verdicts, and neither of those questions had ever been asked. A gap makes no noise.

Which means the push is what found them. This is the part I keep turning over. I spent the week building something so I would not have to trust anybody's word, then pushed sixteen fixes past it in one night — and that night is when the real bugs surfaced. Not because rushing works. Because putting it in front of a person is a kind of test that no amount of measuring replaces.

So: slowly beats rushing, and I would say that again. Every check was written because something real broke, and none of them can be argued with. But the thing that caught the two worst faults of the week was a human being opening a panel and taking a picture.

What I would change

The gate answers everything or nothing, and takes two and a half hours to do it. There is no way to ask one small question quickly, which is precisely why I routed around it on Friday night. That is the first thing I would build.

The export service is the other one. We built our own renderer first, with Claude Opus 5, and it worked. Then it moved to a service that runs on somebody else's machine, which was easier to set up and came with limits I did not understand until they stopped me: a cap on how often a browser can start, and a few minutes of drawing a day. For a gallery nobody has heard of yet, that is fine. For five people exporting videos at once, it is unusable.

The plan is to keep the hosted service as a fallback and move the drawing onto a small rented machine — a few pounds a month, no daily cap, and it gets slower when it is busy instead of refusing. That is the behaviour you want when real people are using something.

And the page itself

It will never be finished, which I have made peace with. It cannot tell me whether something looks good. It cannot run the slow things any faster. It did not watch itself until I made it, and it still goes quiet for the two and a half hours when I most want to know what is happening.

But it does one thing completely: it will not let a result outlive the thing it judged, and it will not let "didn't run" wear the same colour as "passed". For six days of twelve agents at a time, that turned out to be enough to finish on.

What I would tell someone starting

  • Write the rule down before the agents start. One page describing what "correct" means is what turns twelve agents from twelve arguments into an afternoon's work.
  • Ask where you can see the state, early. That page took forty minutes and changed how the rest of the week ran. Every "where are we?" you answer in conversation, you pay for twice.
  • Talking is the expensive part. Work described message by message costs more than work written down and handed over — even to twelve agents at once.
  • Give every agent its own files. Where you can't, put it in writing. Two agents on one file both report success and one of them is wrong.
  • Watch the laptop, not just the bill. Twelve agents and 57 browsers are both real costs, and only one of them shows up on an invoice.
  • Break a check on purpose and watch it fail. A check you have never seen fail is decoration.
  • Make "didn't run" impossible to mistake for "passed". They are opposite facts and must never share a colour, a word or a sentence.
  • Keep your own eyes in it. A ring that didn't wrap its planet, a sun below its own horizon and a card with its numbers hanging off all passed every check that existed.

Six days, 135 models, 125 helper agents and eleven checks, spent almost entirely on making one gallery look the same everywhere — and the last word, every single time, went to a person looking at the screen and asking what something meant.

Postscript: the laptop did not get a day off

One detail I have left out, which is really the whole reason I noticed the resource problem at all.

I could not play anything all week.

The machine is an ASUS TUF A14 from 2024. It is a gaming laptop. For six days it was a render farm. Twelve agents in the afternoon, fifty-seven browsers one evening, and a checking run that takes sixteen gigabytes of memory down to under two and holds it there for two and a half hours.

I would glance over and find it at full fan, most of its memory gone, patiently drawing a bookshelf for the four hundredth time.

Then there were the nights. More than once I got up in the small hours purely to check whether the run had stopped — not because anything needed doing, but because a run that dies at two in the morning will sit there claiming to be running until you wake up, and that costs you the entire night. That is the twenty-hour lie the page told once, and it is why the status tool now says "I could not look" instead of "nothing is running".

So: six days, 135 models, eleven checks, 125 helper agents — and one gaming laptop that played no games and was not switched off once.

It has a fan and no feelings, so it is fine. But if you are planning a week like this one, budget for the machine as well as the bill. And possibly do not run it on the computer you were hoping to relax with.
